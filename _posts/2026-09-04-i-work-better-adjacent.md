I keep telling people that I don't work well on a team.

And I think I've been saying that wrong.

Because whenever I say it, it causes confusion. And it should. I've worked on teams for basically my entire career. I've managed teams. I've worked across teams. I've built things with other people. I run a conference with my wife.

I clearly work well with other people.

So what am I actually trying to say?

I think it's that I work better **adjacent**.

## I don't know what I'm doing

I mean that in a very specific way.

Usually, I know where I want to end up.

I know what I'm capable of. I know my limits. I have a decent idea of which limits I can push. And I'm usually pretty confident that I'll figure out some way to get where I'm trying to go.

I just have absolutely no idea what the steps are going to be.

The destination is clear.

The journey isn't.

I'll try something. It won't work. I'll learn something. I'll go in a completely different direction. I'll see somebody else doing something that seems unrelated and realize that part of their solution applies to my problem.

Sometimes I'll build the wrong thing for a while before realizing what the right thing even is.

And I'm completely comfortable with that.

What I'm not very good at is explaining that process before it happens.

If you ask me what I'm going to do next week, I can probably tell you what I want to have accomplished.

If you ask me exactly how I'm going to accomplish it, my answer is probably:

I don't know yet.

## I don't actually want to work alone

This is the part I think I've misunderstood about myself.

I don't want to disappear into a room by myself and emerge three months later with something finished.

I actually really like having people around me.

More importantly, I like having **different kinds of people** around me.

Different teams. Different domains. Different disciplines. Different problems.

I like seeing what other people are working on. I like learning how they approach things. I like hearing about a problem that has absolutely nothing to do with mine and realizing that it somehow gives me an idea for mine.

Those things become inputs.

They change how I think about the destination. They give me shortcuts. They send me down completely different paths.

I don't necessarily want everyone walking the same path with me.

I want to be adjacent to their paths.

## This probably explains all of my side projects

I make a lot of things on the side.

Indie apps. Tools. Experiments. Random ideas that become slightly less random ideas.

And they're fun.

Nobody needs me to explain what I'm doing.

I can just do it.

I can spend a few hours building something, decide it's terrible, throw it away, and start over. I don't have to explain why the thing I'm working on today isn't the thing I thought I'd be working on yesterday.

There isn't really a plan to deviate from.

There's just somewhere I'm trying to get.

And I've noticed something else over the years:

**My side quests have a habit of becoming main quests.**

Something starts as an experiment off to the side. There's less pressure around it. I can explore it however I want. I can pull ideas from whatever I'm seeing around me.

Then eventually it works.

Or it solves a real problem.

Or other people start using it.

And suddenly the thing I was doing on the side isn't really on the side anymore.

## Deep Dish Swift works like this too

[Deep Dish Swift](https://deepdishswift.com/) is obviously much bigger than one of my random indie apps, but the process feels surprisingly similar.

My wife and I know what we're trying to make.

We know what we want those few days in April to feel like.

Then we work backwards.

We break things down. We figure things out. We change things. Something goes wrong. Something unexpected works. We learn something from a sponsor or speaker or attendee or venue or another conference and adjust.

There are obviously deadlines and things that absolutely have to happen.

But there has never been a perfect map from "we should make a conference" to hundreds of people showing up in Chicago.

We just know where we're going.

Then we figure out how to get there.

## There were times management gave me this too

I've also been thinking about the times being an engineering manager at [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/) worked well for me.

I don't think I'd say that I worked well as an engineering manager overall. But there were definitely periods where the shape of the job lined up really well with how I work.

I had an amazing team.

They didn't need me to figure out everyone's path for them. We could agree on where we were trying to go, and they were extremely capable of figuring out how to get there.

And during some of those periods, I was still doing a lot of IC-ish work around the edges.

Side quests.

I could jump into something technical. Help unblock something. Explore an idea. Work across teams. Notice something happening somewhere else and connect it back to what we were doing.

I was on one team, but my work could be adjacent to a lot of things.

Those were some of the times management worked really well for me.

Not necessarily because management itself was the perfect role for me.

But because, for a while, it gave me the kind of work I seem to keep finding anyway.

## Maybe DevEx is just side quests as a job

I'm in Developer Experience now, and I've realized something kind of funny about my new role.

A lot of my job is side quests.

I'm on an awesome team, but we're not all doing the same thing.

We're working in different areas. We're solving different problems. We're bringing different skills to them.

And I get to move between teams, domains, problems, and disciplines.

I get to see what people are doing.

I get to learn from it.

I get to connect things.

I get to find some weird problem off to the side and follow it for a while to see where it goes.

And sometimes that side quest turns out to be pretty important.

For a long time, I've described myself as someone who doesn't work well on a team.

I don't think that's true anymore.

I don't work well alone.

I don't work poorly with teams.

**I work well adjacent.**

Give me a destination.

Give me good people doing interesting things around me.

And give me enough room to figure out how I'm going to get there.
